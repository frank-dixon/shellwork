Color

Pigment on the shell

Egg color lives in the shell, not the yolk diet myth. Brown eggs wear a pigment called protoporphyrin. Blue-green eggs carry biliverdin (sometimes called oocyan). Layer both and you get olive tones.

In the shell gland, brown-egg layers deposit protoporphyrin IX onto the cuticle and outer shell late in formation. Blue-egg breeds produce biliverdin that tints the shell matrix earlier, so the blue reads through the whole thickness. When a hen carries both pathways, brown over blue yields green or olive shells. White eggs simply lack those deposits — the calcium carbonate shows through.

Shape

Round, classic, pointed

Most eggs are gently oval: a blunter end and a slightly sharper tip. Shape varies by hen and breed. Sudden runs of odd shapes can flag stress or health issues, but everyday variation is normal.

Poultry scientists often summarize shape with a shape index (width divided by length, times 100). Values near the mid-70s describe a classic table egg; lower numbers read more elongated. The shell gland and membranes set that silhouette hours before lay. Extreme misshapes deserve a flock check; mild scatter does not.

Size

Weight classes you can feel

Grocery cartons sort eggs by weight per dozen. A Large egg is about two ounces. Younger hens usually start smaller; many breeds settle into Medium or Large as the flock matures.

USDA size names (Peewee through Jumbo) are minimum net weights per dozen, not breed names. Egg weight rises with hen age and body weight, and it responds to nutrition and lighting — so size is a flock-management signal as much as a genetic one. This issue does not predict cross outcomes; it only maps what the carton already claims.
