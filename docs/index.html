Color

Egg + hen, paired

Shell color comes from the hen, not from what she ate that week. Pick a breed tab to see her photo, comb and plumage, body type, legs, and the egg she is known for — white, tinted, brown, blue, or olive.

Brown shells wear protoporphyrin IX deposited late in the shell gland onto the outer shell and cuticle. Blue-green shells carry biliverdin (oocyan) earlier in the matrix, so the tint shows through the full thickness. Layer brown over blue and you get olive. White eggs simply lack those pigments — calcium carbonate shows through. Breed traits below (comb type, earlobes, muffs, shank color) help you recognize the bird that owns each pigment story.

White egg · White Leghorn

Mediterranean layer

  • Comb / wattles: tall bright-red single comb; neat red wattles.
  • Plumage: sleek pure white; tight feathering.
  • Body / posture: light, upright, alert — built to lay, not bulk.
  • Legs: clean yellow shanks; white earlobes (classic white-egg cue).

No brown or blue pigment is deposited, so the calcium shell reads chalk-white. Leghorns are the textbook white-egg commercial bird.

White earlobes correlate strongly (not perfectly) with white eggs in Mediterranean breeds. Absence of protoporphyrin and biliverdin leaves an untinted calcite shell. Thin shells on high producers still need calcium management — color and shell strength are separate traits.

Tinted egg · Buff Orpington

Heavy dual-purpose

  • Comb / wattles: medium red single comb; soft red wattles.
  • Plumage: dense warm buff / light brown; fluffy outline.
  • Body / posture: broad, low, broody-looking; calm carriage.
  • Legs: sturdy clean shanks tucked under a heavy frame.

Light brown or cream “tinted” eggs sit between chalk white and deep mahogany. Orpingtons are a gentle dual-purpose bird whose shells usually read softly colored, not stark white.

Tinted shells carry a lighter load of protoporphyrin than dark brown layers. The pigment still arrives late in shell formation, so the tint is strongest near the surface. Heavy body type pairs with Large–XL eggs once the hen matures — color intensity and egg weight are not the same gene story.

Brown egg · Rhode Island Red

American dual-purpose

  • Comb / wattles: bright red single comb with clear points; matching wattles.
  • Plumage: rich mahogany to golden-brown; dense layered feathers.
  • Body / posture: medium, plump, purposeful stride on pasture.
  • Legs: clean yellowish shanks; red earlobes (brown-egg cue).

Brown eggs wear a pigment called protoporphyrin on the outer shell. Rhode Island Reds and related production browns are the classic American brown-egg bird you meet in pasture photos.

Protoporphyrin IX is deposited in the shell gland late in formation, mostly on the cuticle and outer calcite. That is why brown can rub slightly and why the interior of a brown shell often looks whiter. Red earlobes track with brown eggs in many breeds. Speckling is extra surface pigment, not dirt by default.

Blue egg · Ameraucana

Blue-egg standard breed

  • Comb / wattles: small red pea comb; muffs and beard hide the face line.
  • Plumage: here, blue-laced grey with charcoal edges; several APA colors exist.
  • Body / posture: medium, sturdy; full cheeks from facial feathering.
  • Legs: often slate; paired with the blue-egg genetic package.

Blue eggs get their color from biliverdin in the shell itself. Ameraucanas (muffs, beard, pea comb) are a recognized blue-egg breed — different from rumpless tufted Araucanas and from mixed “Easter Eggers.”

Biliverdin is incorporated earlier, so blue tints the full shell thickness — crack one open and the interior stays blue-green. The oocyan locus is dominant for blue. True Ameraucanas follow a breed standard; backyard blue layers without standard type are usually Easter Eggers. Do not confuse muffs/beard with Araucana ear tufts.

Olive egg · Olive Egger

Blue × dark-brown cross

  • Comb / wattles: often a pea comb; muffs and beard common on Egger types.
  • Plumage: copper to mahogany with darker lace; highly variable.
  • Body / posture: medium dual-purpose look; active pasture stride.
  • Legs: slate or willow shanks often travel with the blue-egg side.

Olive eggs happen when blue-shell genetics meet dark brown pigment — brown over blue reads green. “Olive Egger” is a cross description, not a closed breed, so hens vary while the egg color logic stays the same.

Structurally you still have biliverdin in the matrix plus protoporphyrin on top. Darker brown parents (for example Marans lines) deepen the olive; lighter brown parents give softer sage. This Phase 1 page explains the pigment stack — it does not compute a mating table.

Shape

Round, classic, pointed

Most table eggs are gently oval: a blunt end and a slightly sharper tip. Breed and hen age nudge that silhouette. Sudden runs of very odd shapes can flag stress or health issues; everyday scatter is normal flock variation.

Shape index (width ÷ length × 100) near the mid-70s describes a classic table egg; lower numbers read more elongated. Membranes and the shell gland set the form hours before lay. Light Mediterranean birds can look a touch more elongated; heavy dual-purpose hens often look rounder. Extreme misshapes deserve a flock check — this page is a reading guide, not a diagnosis tool.

Leghorn cue

Sleek, upright body; eggs often a clean classic oval rather than a squat sphere.

Orpington cue

Broad, low posture; shells can read a touch rounder beside the nest straw.

Rhode Island Red cue

Medium dual-purpose frame; brown eggs usually sit in the everyday oval band.

Size

Weight classes, breed norms

Carton names are weight per dozen. A Large egg is about two ounces. Light Mediterranean breeds such as White Leghorns often sit Medium–Large; heavier dual-purpose hens like Orpingtons and mature Rhode Island Reds push Large and Extra Large as they age.

USDA size names are minimum net weights per dozen, not genetic labels. Egg weight rises with hen age and body weight and responds to nutrition and day length. Breed sets a typical band — Leghorns stay relatively light; Orpingtons and other heavy dual-purpose birds trend heavier — but this issue does not predict cross outcomes.
